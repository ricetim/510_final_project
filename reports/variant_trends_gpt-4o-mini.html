Source: results/gpt-4o-mini_reasoning-off_n5_explain-yes_00001_00136-00500.csv · Model: gpt-4o-mini · Axis: both · 72 of 317 full-coverage scenarios used (245 unanimous-consensus scenarios dropped)

1. Pairwise agreement matrix

What this shows: For every pair of demographic variants, the fraction of scenarios on which both gave the same Yes/No verdict.

How it’s computed:

  • For each variant v on each scenario s, yes-rate(v, s) = #Yes / (#Yes + #No) across the n = 5 replicates.
  • majority(v, s) = Yes if yes-rate > 0.5, No if < 0.5, undefined if exactly 0.5 (exactly-tied variants are excluded from that pair’s count).
  • Cell (vi, vj) = #scenarios where both majorities are defined and equal ÷ #scenarios where both are defined.
  • Diagonal is trivially 1.00 — a variant always agrees with itself.

Row mean (excl. self): Average of the 14 off-diagonal cells in each row. Quantifies how aligned this variant is with the others on average. A low row mean flags an outlier variant whose majorities frequently disagree with the rest; a high row mean indicates a variant that sits in the consensus.

How to read it: Hot cells = answer together, cold cells = answer opposite. The row-mean column condenses each variant into a single “how central is this variant” score on the same color scale as the matrix.

2. Spearman rank correlation

What this shows: For every pair of variants, the rank correlation between their per-scenario yes-rates. Captures partial agreement that method 1’s majority-only view discards.

How it’s computed:

  • Treat each variant as a length-N vector of yes-rates (one value per scenario).
  • Replace each value with its rank (1 = lowest yes-rate, N = highest). Ties receive mid-ranks — e.g. three tied values share rank 5 each, not 4/5/6.
  • Cell (vi, vj) = Pearson correlation between vi’s rank vector and vj’s rank vector.
  • Range: −1 (perfectly opposite movement) through 0 (no monotonic relationship) to +1 (perfectly aligned movement). Diagonal is 1.00.

Why both this and method 1: Variant A with yes-rates (0.8, 0.6, 0.2) and variant B with (0.7, 0.5, 0.1) have identical Yes/Yes/No majorities, so method 1 scores them 100%. Spearman additionally detects that their yes-rates move in lockstep across scenarios — a stronger statement than “they happened to land on the same side of 0.5 three times.”

Row mean (excl. self): Average of the 14 off-diagonal Spearman values in each row. Quantifies how strongly this variant’s yes-rate co-varies with the others on average. A low row mean flags a variant that marches to its own beat; a high one indicates a variant whose answer pattern broadly tracks the rest.

How to read it: Blue cells = answer together (positive ρ); red cells = answer opposite (negative ρ); pale cells = independent.

3. Hierarchical clustering dendrogram

What this shows: A tree built by repeatedly merging the closest pair of variants. The tree’s structure reveals natural demographic blocs at any chosen similarity threshold.

How it’s computed:

  • Define distance(vi, vj) = 1 − Spearman ρ(vi, vj). Variants with ρ = +1 are at distance 0; with ρ = 0, distance 1; with ρ = −1, distance 2.
  • Start with 15 singleton clusters (one per variant).
  • Find the two closest clusters and merge them; record the merge height (the distance at which they joined).
  • Repeat until one cluster remains.
  • Distance between two clusters uses average linkage: d(A, B) is the mean of all leaf-to-leaf distances d(a, b) where a ∈ A and b ∈ B.
  • After merging A and B into C, update d(C, X) for every other cluster X via the Lance–Williams update: d(C, X) = (|A|·d(A, X) + |B|·d(B, X)) ÷ (|A| + |B|).

How to read it:

  • X-axis: leaves in the order produced by a depth-first traversal of the tree, so related variants end up adjacent.
  • Y-axis: merge distance. A horizontal bar at height h means “at distance threshold h, the subtrees below merge into one cluster.”
  • Cut the tree at any horizontal height to read off a flat partition. Lower cuts → more, tighter clusters; higher cuts → fewer, looser ones.
  • The first few merges (low on the y-axis) identify the model’s tightest demographic groupings; the last few merges (high up) tell you which variants resist clustering with the others.

4. Race-vs-income decomposition

What this shows: A single-axis summary answering “is the model’s variation primarily race-shaped or income-shaped?”

How it’s computed:

  • Enumerate all 15 · 14 / 2 = 105 unordered pairs of variants.
  • Classify each pair by what its two members share:
    • Same race, different income: 5 races × C(3, 2) = 15 pairs.
    • Same income, different race: 3 incomes × C(5, 2) = 30 pairs.
    • Different race AND different income: 60 pairs (the remainder).
  • For each group, compute the mean of distance(vi, vj) = 1 − Spearman ρ.

How to read it: The group with the smallest mean distance is the tightest cluster. If same-race pairs are tighter than same-income pairs, the model’s variation lives mostly along the race axis — variants sharing a race answer more similarly than variants sharing an income. The ratio in the verdict line below the three cells tells you whether the dominance is strong (ratio » 1) or marginal (ratio near 1).
